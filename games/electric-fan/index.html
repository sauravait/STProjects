The Electric Motor

At the heart of every fan is an induction motor. When AC current flows through the stator coils, it creates a rotating magnetic field that drags the rotor along.

  • Stator (Fixed): Outer coils carry AC current, producing a rotating magnetic field. The coil poles glow as current alternates.
  • Rotor (Rotating): Squirrel-cage conductors have currents induced in them by the changing field. The resulting force (F = BIL) spins the shaft.
  • Magnetic Field: N→S field lines sweep around the rotor 50 times per second (50 Hz), dragging it at slightly less speed (slip).
F = B · I · L

F = force  ·  B = magnetic flux density  ·  I = induced current  ·  L = conductor length

Blade Pitch & Airflow Physics

Blades are tilted at a pitch angle θ ≈ 15–20°. As they rotate, their shape deflects air rearward — the reaction force pushes air forward toward you.

LowMediumHigh
📐
Pitch Angle (θ)

A steeper angle moves more air per rotation but increases motor torque demand. Optimal: 15–20°.

🌀
Number of Blades

More blades ↑ airflow, but ↑ drag. 3–5 blades balance airflow against efficiency.

Scene 4 of 6

Speed Control Physics

Fan speed is determined by the voltage applied to the motor. Reducing voltage reduces the rotating magnetic field strength, lowering rotor speed.

🎛️
Resistive Regulator

A series resistor drops voltage before the motor. Simple but dissipates wasted power as heat (Ploss = I²R).

⚡
Capacitor / TRIAC Regulator

Capacitors limit AC current without resistive loss. TRIAC phase-cuts the sine wave to vary effective voltage.

🔢
BLDC + Inverter

Brushless DC motors use PWM inverters for stepless, ultra-efficient speed control. Zero brushes = no wear.

Energy & Evaporative Cooling

A fan does not lower air temperature. It accelerates evaporation of sweat from your skin. Each gram of water evaporated absorbs ~2260 J of heat.
